The Panchang: Understanding the Five Limbs of the Vedic Calendar

8 min read · By AstroHand Editorial Team · Updated 2026-07-21

Every day in the traditional Hindu world has a quality — a texture and a fitness for certain activities — and that quality is described by the Panchang, the Vedic almanac. The word means "five limbs" (*pancha* = five, *anga* = limb), because a complete Panchang is built from five elements that together define the astrological character of any given day. For festivals, fasts, ceremonies, and the everyday question of *is today a good day for this?*, the Panchang is the reference the tradition has used for millennia.

What the Panchang Is

The Panchang is a daily calendar computed from the real positions of the Sun and Moon. Unlike the fixed Gregorian calendar, it is a luni-solar system: it tracks both the solar day and the Moon's constantly shifting position, producing a rich description of each day's spiritual and practical quality. The five limbs — tithi, vaara, nakshatra, yoga, and karana — are calculated from the changing angular relationship between the Sun and Moon, so a genuine Panchang requires real astronomical computation, not a lookup table. The Five Limbs

1. Tithi — the lunar day. The tithi is defined by the angular distance between the Sun and Moon, in steps of twelve degrees. There are thirty tithis in a lunar month — fifteen in the waxing fortnight (Shukla Paksha), from new Moon to full Moon, and fifteen in the waning fortnight (Krishna Paksha), from full Moon back to new. Each tithi has a character and a ruling deity, and certain tithis favour certain activities. The "empty" Rikta tithis — the fourth (Chaturthi), ninth (Navami), and fourteenth (Chaturdashi) — are traditionally avoided for auspicious beginnings, while others like the fifth (Panchami) or the full-Moon Purnima are considered favourable. The tithi is the beating heart of the Panchang, governing festivals, fasts (like Ekadashi), and muhurat.

2. Vaara — the weekday. The seven days of the week, each ruled by a planet: Sunday (Sun), Monday (Moon), Tuesday (Mars), Wednesday (Mercury), Thursday (Jupiter), Friday (Venus), and Saturday (Saturn). The ruling planet lends its quality to the day, which is why certain worship and activities are traditionally assigned to certain weekdays — Hanuman on Tuesday and Saturday, Lakshmi on Friday, and so on.

3. Nakshatra — the lunar mansion. The nakshatra the Moon occupies on a given day, out of the twenty-seven. Because each nakshatra has its own character and ruling deity, the Moon's nakshatra strongly shapes the day's quality and its fitness for particular activities. Muhurat selection leans heavily on choosing a suitable nakshatra for the task at hand.

4. Yoga — the luni-solar combination. The yoga is calculated from the *sum* of the Sun's and Moon's longitudes, producing twenty-seven yogas, each with a name and quality. Some yogas are auspicious, some inauspicious, adding another layer to the day's character. (These calendar yogas are distinct from the planetary yogas found in a birth chart.)

5. Karana — the half-tithi. A karana is half of a tithi, so there are two karanas per lunar day and sixty in a lunar month, drawn from a set of eleven repeating types. The karana adds fine detail to the timing of activities within a day.

How the Panchang Guides Life

Together, these five limbs answer the practical question the tradition cares about: *what is the quality of this day, and what is it suited for?* A day with an auspicious tithi, a favourable nakshatra, a benefic weekday, and a good yoga is ideal for important beginnings. A day carrying a Rikta tithi or an inauspicious nakshatra is one to avoid for weddings, house-warmings, or major ventures — though ordinary life, of course, goes on regardless.

The Panchang also fixes the festival calendar. Diwali, Holi, Navratri, Ekadashi fasts, and countless observances are all timed by tithi and nakshatra, not by the fixed solar date — which is why these festivals fall on different Gregorian dates each year. To know when a festival truly falls, one consults the Panchang.

Inauspicious Windows

Beyond the five limbs, a Panchang typically notes daily inauspicious time windows to avoid for important work — most famously Rahu Kaal, a roughly ninety-minute period each day (its timing varies by weekday and by sunrise), along with Yamaganda and Gulika Kaal. Traditional practice avoids starting significant new ventures during these windows, treating them as small daily "shadows."

Using the Panchang Today

For most people today, the Panchang is most useful for two things: knowing when festivals and fasts fall, and checking the quality of a day before an important beginning. It pairs naturally with Choghadiya and muhurat selection — the Panchang gives the day's overall character, while Choghadiya divides the day into favourable and unfavourable slots. Together they let you align important actions with a supportive rhythm.

Held wisely, the Panchang is a beautiful inheritance — a way of experiencing time not as uniform and mechanical but as living, textured, and meaningful.
