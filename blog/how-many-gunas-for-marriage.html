How Many Gunas Should Match for Marriage? Guna Milan Explained

7 min read · By AstroHand Editorial Team · Updated 2026-07-24

When two horoscopes are matched for marriage in the Vedic tradition, the result is famously a number out of 36. This score comes from Ashtakoota Guna Milan — an eight-fold system that compares the birth stars (nakshatras) of the bride and groom across eight factors, or kootas. The single most common question families ask is simple: how many gunas should match for a marriage to be considered good? The honest answer is a little more nuanced than one cut-off, and understanding it saves a great deal of unnecessary worry.

The eight kootas and their points

The 36 points are spread across eight kootas, each weighing a different dimension of compatibility:

- Varna (1 point) — spiritual compatibility and ego balance.
- Vashya (2 points) — mutual attraction and influence.
- Tara (3 points) — health and well-being.
- Yoni (4 points) — physical and intimate compatibility.
- Graha Maitri (5 points) — mental and psychological rapport.
- Gana (6 points) — temperament and nature.
- Bhakoot (7 points) — love, family welfare and prosperity.
- Nadi (8 points) — health, genetic compatibility and progeny.

Add them and the maximum is 36. Notice the points are not equal — Nadi and Bhakoot alone carry 15 of the 36, which is why they weigh so heavily in interpretation.

How many gunas are considered good?

Traditional guidelines read roughly as follows:

- Below 18 gunas: generally not recommended on the score alone, unless other strong factors balance it.
- 18 to 24 gunas: acceptable. This is the most common range for real couples, and such marriages are considered workable and often happy.
- 25 to 32 gunas: very good — strong compatibility across most kootas.
- 32 to 36 gunas: excellent, and quite rare. So rare that some astrologers treat a near-perfect score with mild caution.

So the widely used threshold is 18 gunas — at or above it, a match is traditionally considered suitable. But the score is a starting point, not a verdict.

Why the raw number isn't the whole story

Two matches can both score 22 and yet be very different, because what matters is *which* kootas earned the points. In particular:

- Nadi Dosha: if both partners share the same Nadi, the 8 Nadi points score zero. This is taken seriously as it relates to health and progeny — though classical texts also list several conditions under which Nadi dosha is cancelled (Nadi Dosha Parihar).
- Bhakoot Dosha: certain sign combinations lose all 7 Bhakoot points, affecting harmony and prosperity — again, with recognised cancellations.

A match of 30 that fails Nadi is often considered weaker than a match of 20 that passes it. This is exactly why a raw number without a careful reading can mislead.

What to do if the score is low

A score below 18 is not an automatic "no." The first step is to see *why* it is low — a weak Varna or Vashya matters far less than a failed Nadi or Bhakoot. Next, an astrologer checks for cancellations (parihar): many Nadi and Bhakoot doshas are formally nullified when specific conditions are met, such as a shared sign lord or particular nakshatra relationships. The Navamsa (D9) chart is then examined, since a strong D9 can support a marriage that the raw Ashtakoota score undersells. Only after all of this does a low number carry real weight. Families who understand these steps tend to worry far less about a single figure.

Where Mangal Dosha fits in

Guna Milan does not include Mangal (Manglik) Dosha — that is checked separately, from the position of Mars. A couple can score a high Guna Milan and still need a Mangal Dosha review, and here too the dosha is frequently cancelled or mutually balanced when both partners are Manglik. Fear of the word "Manglik" breaks far more matches than the astrology warrants.

A balanced, honest view

Guna Milan is a valuable screening tool, but marriages are not made or broken by a number. A modest score can be strengthened by a strong Navamsa (D9) chart, favourable dashas, and above all the maturity of two people. A high score is a good sign, not a guarantee. The wisest use of Ashtakoota is as one input among several, read in full context rather than in fear.
