Choghadiya and Muhurat: How to Pick an Auspicious Time the Vedic Way

5 min read · AstroHand Editorial

Long before appointment apps, Indian tradition carried a portable scheduling system: Choghadiya. The idea is elegantly simple. The time between sunrise and sunset is divided into eight equal parts, and the night from sunset to the next sunrise into eight more. Each part — roughly ninety minutes, though it stretches and shrinks with the seasons — carries one of seven qualities that rotate in a fixed sequence anchored to the weekday.

The Seven Qualities

Four are considered favourable. Amrit ("nectar") is the best of all, suited to any good work. Shubh ("auspicious") favours ceremonies, worship, and beginnings. Labh ("gain") is the merchant’s favourite — ideal for business, deals, and new ventures. Char ("moving") supports travel and anything meant to keep moving.

Three are avoided for new beginnings. Udveg ("anxiety") brings restlessness — government and paperwork are sometimes excepted. Kaal ("time/death") is considered inauspicious for starts, though some traditions permit wealth-accumulating routine work. Rog ("illness") is avoided for journeys and launches, though it is said to suit confrontation — an hour for the courtroom, not the wedding.

Why the Same Slot Differs by City

Because the system is anchored to actual sunrise and sunset, Choghadiya is inherently local. Mumbai’s Amrit is not Delhi’s Amrit. A table printed for one city is wrong for another by many minutes — which is why serious Panchang tools compute sun times for your exact location rather than reprinting a generic list. Day slots in summer are long and night slots short; winter reverses them.

Choghadiya vs Muhurat

Choghadiya is the everyday layer of electional astrology — quick guidance for starting a journey, opening a shop for the day, or making a call. Major life events — weddings, griha pravesh, mundan — use the fuller Muhurat system, which weighs tithi, nakshatra, yoga, karana, and planetary positions together. Think of Choghadiya as choosing a good hour and Muhurat as choosing a good day; for important matters, tradition uses both.

Using It Well

The healthiest way to use Choghadiya is as a rhythm, not a cage. If a task must happen in Udveg, tradition itself offers workarounds — begin the mental work in a good slot, or take the first symbolic step earlier. The system was always meant to serve life, not stall it.
