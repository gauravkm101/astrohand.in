Vivah Muhurat: How an Auspicious Wedding Date Is Chosen

7 min read · By AstroHand Editorial Team · Updated 2026-07-24

In the Vedic tradition, a marriage is not fixed on a convenient weekend but on a vivah muhurat — a carefully chosen moment when the heavens are considered favourable for beginning married life. The belief is simple and hopeful: that starting a lifelong union at an auspicious time gives it the best possible cosmic footing. Understanding how a muhurat is selected demystifies the process and helps families plan with confidence.

The building blocks of a wedding muhurat

A vivah muhurat is chosen by weighing several elements of the Panchang together:

- Tithi (lunar day): Auspicious tithis are preferred, while the Rikta tithis (the 4th, 9th and 14th) and Amavasya are generally avoided for marriage.
- Nakshatra: Certain stars are traditionally considered favourable for marriage — Rohini, Mrigashira, Magha, Uttara Phalguni, Hasta, Swati, Anuradha, Moola, Uttara Ashadha, Uttara Bhadrapada and Revati.
- Vaar (weekday): Monday, Wednesday, Thursday and Friday are usually preferred; Tuesday (ruled by Mars) is often avoided.
- Lagna (rising sign) at the ceremony: A strong, benefic ascendant at the moment vows are exchanged is considered important.

Periods when marriages are traditionally paused

Just as some times are chosen, others are set aside entirely:

- Chaturmas: the roughly four-month period when Lord Vishnu is said to sleep (from Devshayani Ekadashi to Devuthani Ekadashi) is traditionally free of weddings.
- Malmas (Adhik Maas): the extra lunar month added periodically is avoided for auspicious ceremonies.
- Kharmas: the roughly month-long periods when the Sun transits Sagittarius and Pisces.
- Tara Doob: when Jupiter (Guru) or Venus (Shukra) is combust — too close to the Sun — marriages are paused, since these are the natural significators of marriage.

Why the couple's own charts matter most

A calendar muhurat that is good "in general" is only half the picture. The most careful astrologers also study the bride's and groom's own horoscopes — the strength of the seventh house (marriage), the condition of Venus and Jupiter, the dasha periods each is running, and any Mangal Dosha. A date that is broadly auspicious but clashes with a couple's personal charts is less valuable than one chosen to suit them specifically. This is why a good muhurat is personalised, not simply picked from a list.

Fallback muhurats when the perfect date isn't possible

Not every family can align a wedding with a rare, ideal date — venues, guests and seasons all impose limits, and Vedic astrology allows for this. The Abhijit muhurat, a short and highly auspicious window around midday, is a trusted fallback when a fuller muhurat cannot be arranged. And whenever a broadly favourable date is chosen, the astrologer still selects a strong ascendant for the exact moment of the pheras (the vows around the sacred fire), since that instant is treated as the true beginning of the marriage. In short, even within a crowded calendar there is almost always a way to find a dignified, auspicious moment.

Common questions families ask

- Does the muhurat have to be at night? No. Pheras are performed at whatever auspicious time is calculated, day or night; the "night wedding" is a regional custom, not a rule.
- What if we can't marry in the auspicious months? Registry formalities can be completed anytime — it is the ceremonial pheras that are timed to the muhurat.
- Do both families' traditions matter? Yes. A careful astrologer harmonises the couple's charts and, where customs differ, finds a window acceptable to both sides.

Keeping it in perspective

A muhurat is meant to bless a beginning, not to guarantee an outcome. A wedding held at a perfect time still depends on two people to make the marriage work, and countless happy marriages have begun on ordinary days. Treat the muhurat as a meaningful, hopeful tradition — a way of starting with intention and the goodwill of family — rather than a mechanical determinant of the future.
